## If the tunnel architecture is reinstated

Premises from the October docs (PF §13(a), (c)): one boiler plant in shop Bay 1 serving shop slab, tunnel, house hydronics, pool exchanger and snow melt (Shop-Base-Plan:101, 288-298); a ~70 ft heated tunnel (Shop-Base-Plan:96, 373-376) holding hydronic mains, water, power, fibre and a forcemain; gas direct-buried outside it (Shop-Base-Plan:369). Numbers below use the same unverified 15-25 BTU/h per sq ft rule for the shop and carry the same caveat.

- **Load.** Shop 100 x 100 ft = 10,000 sq ft x 15-25 = 150,000-250,000 BTU/h (44-73 kW), with seven overhead doors (six 14 x 14 ft, one 10 x 12 ft: 1,296 sq ft of door, `shop.scad:68-80`), so door air loss is likely to push it above the rule. Tunnel: about 800 sq ft at 15.5-19 C, "a small load" (Shop-Base-Plan:374); not sized. Single-plant total = house 134,418-224,030 + shop 150,000-250,000 + snow melt (default 110,670; shop apron edges extra) = **395,088-584,700 BTU/h (115.8-171.4 kW)**, before pool standing load, DHW and the tunnel. Both ends are under the NECB's 176 kW line (600,512 BTU/h), the top end by only 3%; pool standing load, DHW, the tunnel and the door air loss would take it over, and above 176 kW the boiler plant must be more than one boiler or staged. Any hot-water loop above 88 kW needs outdoor-temperature reset controls (NECB 5.2.11.4., 5.2.11.5.).
- **Single point of failure.** If the Bay 1 plant stops mid-winter, the house loses space heat, DHW, pool heat and the snow melt together, because the model has no house-side boiler in that architecture. What is at risk first is the water: the tunnel's water lines and the 70 ft hydronic main (the house shell has far more thermal mass); the research puts heat trace on the water "for the one week the heater fails in January" (Shop-Base-Plan:284-286). A minimal house backup (a small electric or gas source in the UTILITY room) and a low-temperature alarm to a phone is the cheapest hedge; two boilers is the NECB-style answer (NECB 5.2.11.4.(1) also requires a way to stop heat loss through idle boilers). The house's own power and gas come from the road in the current model, so the house is not electrically dependent on the shop today: reinstating the tunnel would create the dependency.
- **Freeze risk in the run.** Tunnel temperature is held at 60-66 F (15.5-19 C) by the shop boiler (Shop-Base-Plan:373-376); water mains are insulated and traced; sewage forcemain is in the same tunnel. If the plant and the electric unit heater both fail, the tunnel cools toward ground temperature; the freeze margin is a thermal-time-constant calculation (ground temperature at depth, insulation, tunnel volume) the model cannot give.
- **Gas.** Natural gas stays outside the tunnel by default and enters the house UTILITIES room through its own sleeve (Shop-Base-Plan:369); this needs B149.1 check and the gas inspector's approval if it is ever proposed inside. The meter and regulator location relative to Bay 1 is not modelled. Propane in the tunnel case: one tank near the shop, two buried runs.
- **Shop heating (NECB).** The shop is over the 300 m2 line (10,000 sq ft = 929 m2), so the NECB applies. Options: gas unit heaters (Et >= 82% in NECB Table 5.2.12.1.-O), tubular or high-intensity gas infrared heaters (NRE >= 45% and >= 55%, Table 5.2.12.1.-P), or the Shop-Base-Plan's in-slab hydronic one zone per bay (Shop-Base-Plan:159). The wash bay (25 x 100 ft, 14 ft doors both ends) is a humidity source; the NECB requires an automatic humidity control device wherever the system adds or removes moisture (NECB 5.2.9.1.); a pull-through wash bay with open doors makes tubular infrared or slab heat more sensible than unit heaters, a layout judgement not a code finding. NECB 5.2.8.7. requires shut-down controls on snow-melt heaters at the apron.
- **Parents' house.** If the shop plant also fed the parents' house, the ABC's one-dwelling-unit heating rule would not apply (ABC 9.33.1.1.(2) sends it to Part 6).
